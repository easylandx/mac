Chapter One · Mostly Factual

A brief, slightly unhinged history

Real dates. Real people. One (1) tunic dress that quietly rewrote women's fashion. We are not making this part up — the truth was already ridiculous enough.

1917
A boutique opens in San Sebastián
Cristóbal Balenciaga opens his first shop in Spain, expanding to Madrid and Barcelona. The Spanish royal family and aristocracy become clients — which is a strong start for a man who will later put shoulders where waists used to be.
1937
Paris, because the Spanish Civil War said so
Forced to close his Spanish stores, Balenciaga relocates to Avenue George V in Paris. His first show, heavily influenced by the Spanish Renaissance, is an almost immediate hit. Harper's Bazaar editor Carmel Snow becomes an early champion.
Reinvention, forcibly
1951–59
He simply invents a new body
Broad shoulders, no waist, the tunic dress, the balloon jacket, the baby doll dress, the cocoon coat, the sack dress, the Empire line cut like a kimono. Historians consider it fashion's most important reworking of the female silhouette in the 20th century. No trash bags were involved.
1957
He shows collections the day before delivery
Balenciaga starts revealing his designs to press just one day before pieces ship to stores — instead of the usual four weeks — specifically to stop rivals from copying his work first. The press hates it. He does not care.
Petty, but correct
1968 / 1972
The house closes. The founder dies.
Cristóbal closes his fashion house in 1968 and passes away in 1972. The brand drifts through ownership — his nephews, then the German group Hoechst — waiting for someone to remember how good this was.
1997
Nicolas Ghesquière and the birth of the Motorcycle bag
Appointed Artistic Director, Ghesquière keeps the house's restrained aesthetic while introducing Balenciaga's first true "It" accessory — the Motorcycle bag — and pulls in fans like Madonna along the way.
2001
Kering (then PPR) takes control
The Gucci Group acquires a controlling stake, placing Balenciaga alongside Yves Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta and Boucheron in the same corporate family it still belongs to today.
2015
Demna arrives. Absurdity becomes strategy.
Demna Gvasalia is named creative director and begins deliberately blurring luxury with the mundane: IKEA-style totes, deconstructed sneakers, and price tags that make headlines on purpose. Revenue reportedly approaches $2 billion by 2022.
This is the part you came for
2021
Sweatpants with fake boxers, €1,190
Balenciaga releases trompe-l'œil sweatpants designed to look like they have plaid boxers peeking out the top. They are, in fact, just sweatpants. The internet does not recover.
2025
Pierpaolo Piccioli takes the reins
The Italian designer, previously of Valentino, becomes Creative Director — bringing a reputation for romantic, couture-level craftsmanship to a house currently also famous for a bag shaped like garbage.
2026
You, reading this, right now
282 stores worldwide, one Cristóbal Balenciaga Museum in Getaria, and a new era under Piccioli — still deciding whether to be sincere or spectacular. Probably both, at the same time, on purpose.

Chapter Three · Before It Was A Bit

The founder was already unthinkable — for real reasons

Long before trash-bag totes, Cristóbal Balenciaga was reshaping what clothing could even do to a body. These silhouettes sounded just as strange in their day. They also happened to be genius.

1953

The Balloon Jacket

A jacket cut to billow away from the body entirely, floating around the torso like it forgot it was supposed to be fitted.

1957

The Baby Doll Dress

High-waisted, wide-skirted, and unapologetically shaped nothing like the hourglass everyone else was chasing at the time.

1957

The Sack Dress

Straight-hanging, waistless, and radical enough that its name was meant as an insult. It became one of the most influential shapes of the decade.

1959

The Empire Line

High-waisted coats and dresses cut like kimonos — the culmination of a decade spent quietly moving the waistline wherever he pleased.
