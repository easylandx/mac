站桩

Standing like a post

A quiet posture, a working body, a settling mind — the oldest exercise that asks almost nothing of you but attention.

What it is

You just stand there.

Zhan Zhuang combines standing posture, relaxed breathing, body awareness and meditation. It shows up in qigong and Yiquan, and in parts of Chinese martial arts as foundational training.

At first glance it looks almost too simple to be worth doing. But held correctly, for even a few minutes, it becomes surprisingly demanding — not because of effort, but because of everything effort usually hides.

The posture

Holding the ball

A common starting position, named for the shape the arms make — as if wrapped gently around something round and large.

FeetRoughly shoulder-width apart
KneesSlightly bent — not squatting
PelvisRelaxed, naturally aligned
SpineLengthened, never forced straight
Shoulders & elbowsReleased, hanging soft
ArmsRounded, as though embracing a tree
ChinTucked slightly
TongueResting naturally
BreathQuiet, through the nose
AttentionOn body sensation and breath

Stand with structure, but don't fight gravity.

The key is relaxation without collapsing. You aren't trying to achieve anything spectacular — you remain standing, and you observe what happens.

Why it's difficult

Everything you normally ignore

After a few minutes of stillness, sensation starts arriving in a fairly predictable order:

legs→ shoulders→ lower back→ breathing→ tension→ thoughts→ impatience

The practice is really an exercise in telling the difference between necessary muscular effort and unnecessary tension — the grip you don't need to be holding. Over time the posture tends to become quietly, gradually more effortless.

The meditative side

Moving toward stillness

Zhan Zhuang isn't only a leg exercise. Thoughts will arise — the practice is not to follow them but to return attention to the body: feet, breath, posture, sensation. In that sense it resembles mindfulness meditation, except the body and posture are the primary object, rather than the breath alone.

What you might feel

Especially in the first weeks

Trembling in the legs
Warmth or tingling
Tension through the thighs
Shoulders wanting to rise
One leg carrying more weight
Restlessness, or boredom
Heaviness, lightness, or a sense of bodily integration
Eventually, periods of real calm

None of these are necessary for the practice to be working. They're simply common — worth expecting, not worth chasing.

How long to stand

Duration isn't the point

First sessions
3–5 minutes, rest, then another 3–5 minutes. No need to push further than that.
Once comfortable
Gradually work toward 10–15 minutes in a single stand.
With experience
Some practitioners stand for considerably longer — but a good session can be surprisingly effective at just 10 minutes.

What it's good for

The defensible benefits

There's some evidence that qigong-type practices help with stress, balance, relaxation and general wellbeing — though claims specifically about Zhan Zhuang and extraordinary energy or healing go well beyond what the evidence supports.

Physical

Postural awareness, balance, gentle lower-body endurance, and learning to release muscular tension you don't need.

Mental

Concentration, body awareness, and a calmer nervous system.

Practical

Learning to stay physically relaxed while experiencing discomfort or mental agitation.

One important point

Don't interpret pain as progress.

Mild muscular fatigue or shaking can be normal. Sharp pain, joint pain, numbness, dizziness or significant discomfort is a reason to stop and adjust the posture. The knees especially shouldn't be forced into a deep bend.

The deeper side

Standing that trains power

In Yiquan, standing isn't only a relaxation exercise — it's used to build whole-body coordination, balance and what's often called "internal" power. The practitioner may stand completely still while mentally rehearsing movement: pushing against something enormous, or embracing a tree that pushes gently back.
